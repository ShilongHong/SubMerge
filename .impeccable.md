## Design Context

### Users

SubMerge is used by people who self-host and maintain Clash subscription configurations for themselves or a small team. They are comfortable with subscriptions, proxy groups, and rule syntax, but they should not need to read generated YAML to understand where a rule came from or why it is active. Their main jobs are adding sources, tracing rule ownership, resolving invalid targets or conflicts, and publishing a dependable permanent subscription URL.

The interface must support focused desktop administration as well as quick inspection and small edits from a phone or tablet. Mouse, keyboard, and touch input are all first-class contexts.

### Brand Personality

Calm, precise, trustworthy. SubMerge should feel like a compact operations instrument: direct language, visible system state, conservative use of color, and no decorative effects that compete with configuration data. Success should feel controlled rather than celebratory; warnings and validation should explain the next useful action.

### Aesthetic Direction

Use a refined utilitarian control-console direction with strong information hierarchy and a lightly tinted blue-neutral palette. Both light and dark themes are required. Dense rule data should remain readable through restrained typography, clear alignment, compact status marks, and progressive disclosure instead of nested cards.

The memorable interaction is provenance: selecting a subscription immediately reveals its rules, and every rule visibly carries its source, target, status, and place in the final merge. Avoid generic dashboard metric tiles, glassmorphism, neon gradients, excessive rounding, and motion that does not communicate state.

Desktop should use a three-region master-detail workspace. Tablet should collapse the detail region while preserving the subscription list and rule list. Mobile should use a single-surface drill-down flow with a sticky context header and 44px minimum touch targets. Core functionality must never depend on hover.

### Design Principles

1. Make provenance visible: a user should always know which subscription or system rule set owns a rule.
2. Explain the generated result: validation, deduplication, target mapping, and exclusions must be inspectable before publishing.
3. Preserve user intent across refreshes: remote source rules and local overrides remain separate and clearly labeled.
4. Keep common actions immediate and advanced controls progressive; large rule sets must stay responsive.
5. Protect compatibility and accessibility: v1/v2 behavior remains stable, names including emoji remain exact, themes meet WCAG AA contrast, reduced motion is honored, and all controls work by keyboard and touch.
